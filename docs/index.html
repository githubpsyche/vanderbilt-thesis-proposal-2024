Overview

For my thesis, I propose an investigation into how repetition and similarity across study events influence memory search in free recall tasks, focusing on the role of feature overlap across experiences within the framework of retrieved context theory (RCT) (Howard & Kahana, 2002; Kahana et al., 2022). Implementing variants of the Context Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model of memory search, I’ll conduct and analyze a series of simulations and behavioral experiments to explore how these factors interact to shape the organization and retrieval of memories. This work will clarify the mechanisms underlying repetition and similarity effects in memory, providing a more nuanced and integrated account of how overlapping features in experience influence memory search.

The first chapter introduces and evaluates an instance-based specification of the CMR model, where individual memory traces are encoded for each event and its associated context. Models based on RCT typically use a connectionist approach, where the relationships between items and their contexts are represented by adjustable weights in a network, influencing how activation flows within it (Howard & Kahana, 2002; L. J. Lohnas et al., 2015; Polyn et al., 2009). In contrast, instance models capture each event as a distinct memory trace within an expandable memory matrix, showing versatility across various memory domains like category learning and semantic memory (Cox & Criss, 2020; Hintzman, 1984, 1988; Jamieson et al., 2018; Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997; Turner, 2019). Here we bridge these methodologies by introducing an instance-based version of the Context Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model, which constructs individual memory traces for each item and its contextual associations, driving recall by activating these traces based on their context (Polyn et al., 2009). Our initial simulations confirm that both the instance-based and traditional connectionist versions of CMR are functionally equivalent and equally explain benchmark phenomena in free recall tasks. For my thesis, I propose to further adapt the model to explain semantic contiguity effects and illustrate how RCT can be integrated with other instance-based models. We discuss the implications of these findings for understanding the dynamics of memory search in free recall tasks and the potential for integrating different modeling traditions in cognitive science (Newell, 1973).

In the second chapter, the focus shifts to repeated experience. To investigate the structure of memory for repeated experiences, this study examines how the lag-contiguity effect – the tendency for items positioned close together in a study list to be retrieved successively during free recall (Kahana, 1996) – manifests for items with multiple study positions. Participants will study lists containing both once- and twice-presented items, with the proximity of each presentation of a repeated item to the beginning or end of the study lists varied systematically to probe the influence of serial position effects (Murdock Jr, 1962) on memory trace accessibility. Using a modified lag-conditional response probability (lag-CRP) analysis, we will compare transition probabilities from repeated items to items studied near either their first or second presentation. Preliminary analyses across two archival datasets suggest that, even after accounting for serial position effects, the temporal contiguity effect is more pronounced relative to the first presentation of a repeated item than to the second. Initial results also indicate that the influential Context Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model (Polyn et al., 2009) may not accurately predict these differences, pointing to potential limitations in its current framework (L. L. Lohnas & Kahana, 2014). Further data collection, follow-up analyses, and proposed modeling aim to clarify these findings, enhancing our understanding of memory search and refining theoretical models of memory for repeated experiences.

The final chapter investigates whether the mechanisms underlying repetition and similarity effects in free recall are fundamentally distinct or reflect a continuum over a single underlying process. Despite the intuitive appeal of the continuum hypothesis, an apparent contradiction arises from the contrasting effects of spacing on memory performance: while repeated items benefit from spaced presentation (Cepeda et al., 2006), semantically similar items often show enhanced recall with massed presentation (Borges & Mangler, 1972; Glanzer, 1969; Puff, 1966). Participants will study lists that interleave unrelated items with either twice-presented items or pairs of highly semantically similar items. The design also varies both the spacing between presentations of key items and the proximity of presentations to the beginning or end of lists, enabling investigation of how spacing and serial position effects manifest for different types of item relationships. Applying a retrieved context framework to these data, I will extend traditional measures of repetition effects to study non-repeating but similar word pairs. Analyses will interrogate whether encoding of similar or repeated items triggers the reinstatement of similar context, whether similarity or repetition can modulate the benefit of spaced over massed study for memory performance, and whether these factors similarly interact with serial position to structure response organization. Results will support formal comparison between models that variously treat semantic and repetition effects as engaging distinct or overlapping processes, informing the development of an integrated account of how item and contextual relationships shape memory search in free recall.

This work is planned as part of a broader trajectory of research aimed at developing a more integrated understanding of how relationships between study events in a sequence even beyond basic feature overlap shape the structure of memory. While this thesis focuses on free recall for word lists, parallel and future work will explore how these principles extend to other domains of memory – particularly narrative comprehension where relationships between expressions are fundamental to constructing a coherent mental representation of story content (McNamara & Magliano, 2009). By testing and refining the CMR model’s integrated account of repetition and similarity effects in its typical domain of research, this research will provide a foundation for understanding how relationships between study events shape memory across diverse conditions and tasks.
